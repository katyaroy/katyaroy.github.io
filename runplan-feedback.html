From a runner's email to a release

A focused case inside RunPlan. We have no support team: the email address in Settings reaches me. How I answer runners, what I ask back, and what their emails changed in the app.

My role
Product Designer & Co-Founder — I answer the emails, ask the follow-up questions and build the changes in SwiftUI.
Team
Two people. No support team and no ticket system, just one inbox.

Overview

RunPlan has no accounts, so the one direct line from a runner to us is the email address in Settings. Since late August runners have written in six languages: about a bug, a number that looks wrong, or something they miss. I answer every email, and I treat each one as a short interview that the runner started.

Answer firstThe fix, or a way around the problem today, in plain words
→
Ask backOne or two questions about how they actually run
→
DecideFix it, build it, or say no and explain why
→
Close the loopWrite back when the fix is live

Two bugs, each fixed within a week

Both started with a runner describing exactly what they saw on the screen.

Workouts that recorded nothing

  1. 9 OctA runner writes: the workout starts on the watch, but heart rate, distance and pace stay empty. I send the usual checks for Health permissions.
  2. 10 OctThey have checked everything and attach screenshots. Apple's own Workout app records fine on the same watch. That rules out permissions and sensors, so the problem is ours: RunPlan on watchOS 27.
  3. 13 OctThe fix is in the code.
  4. 14 OctThe fix is on the App Store, and I write back.
  5. 23 OctThe runner confirms it works, and sends a list of further suggestions.

A pace 1.6 times too slow

  1. 4 NovA runner who trains in miles asks why the pace in the splits doesn't match the run. It is a bug: the pace was already per mile and got converted from kilometers a second time. I fix it the same day and explain what went wrong.
  2. 5 NovThe fix is on the App Store.

One question back, and what it changed

A request tells me what a runner wants. A question back tells me how they do it today, and that usually decides the design.

A runner asked forI asked backWhat I learned, and what changed
Kilometers instead of milesWhere do you use miles day to day? In which units do you see your runs in other apps?Miles for driving, kilometers for anything sport. So the phone's region was the wrong signal. Units are now a choice in Settings, on the iPhone and the watch.
Heart-rate zones that fit: Zone 2 felt too lowNothing. I checked our math instead.We took 220 minus age, the roughest method there is. I wrote back: "So it wasn't you – Zone 2 really is too low." Zones are now worked out from maximum and resting heart rate, on their own screen in Settings.
A way to adjust the zonesHow are your zones set today: from maximum and resting heart rate, or as limits for each zone?As limits, from a lab test. So the limits can be typed in as they are, not only calculated.
An Apple Watch complicationNothing. The request was clear.RunPlan on the watch face, and today's run as a card in the Smart Stack.

All four changes shipped on 24 November.

When the answer is no

A runner with time for only two runs a week asked for a half-marathon plan to match. I said no and explained why. Our half-marathon plans start at three runs a week on purpose: with two, the long run becomes most of the running, and too much too soon is how most running injuries happen.

Then I said what works today: the beginner 5K and 10K plans are built for two runs a week. Their own idea, to start earlier and build up more slowly, goes straight at the main risk, and I told them so.

The same question, three times

Three runners asked the same thing in different words: I ran with Apple's Workout app, so why doesn't it count toward my plan? The rule is simple. Every run shows up in Activity and counts in the totals, but only a run started in RunPlan completes a planned session. Nothing in the app says so until it bites.

Three emails about one rule are not three support cases. They are one unclear spot in the interface. What to do about it is open: say the rule earlier, or offer to count an outside run as the day's session.

An email can also arrive before the data. In September a runner wrote that after a few weeks with the app they still couldn't get it onto their watch, so they couldn't start a workout. In November the analytics showed how common that was: Four out of five never reach the watch →

What I learned

  • An email is an interview the runner started. They cared enough to write, so one good question back gets a real answer, often within hours.
  • Ask how they do it today. "Let me adjust my zones" could mean three different screens. The answer, limits from a lab test, picked one.
  • A repeated question is a design finding. The third email about the same rule tells me to fix the interface, not to write a better reply.
